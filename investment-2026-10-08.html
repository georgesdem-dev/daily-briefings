📉 A 24-Year-High Yield Scare Knocks Equities Off Tuesday's Record

After Tuesday's fresh S&P record (~7,822), Wednesday turned risk-off as the 10-year Treasury yield spiked intraday to 5.35-5.36% — its highest print since April 2002 — before a strong 10-year auction pulled it back to roughly 5.28-5.32% by the close. All four major indices fell: S&P -0.22%, Dow -0.66%, Nasdaq -0.22%, and small caps underperformed sharply (Russell 2000 -1.31%) as higher-for-longer rate fears hit rate-sensitive names hardest. VIX actually eased to 15.01, suggesting the move was more a bond-market story than a broad equity panic.

🏦 FOMC Minutes Land Hawkish — But Already Stale Against the Jobs Data

Wednesday's minutes (covering the Sept 15-16 meeting that raised rates 25bp to 3.75-4.00%) showed all 19 officials backed the hike, with most seeing another increase as "likely appropriate by year-end." Markets largely shrugged because the tone is already outdated: last week's weak October jobs report (+29K payrolls, unemployment up to 4.2%) has since collapsed December hike odds from roughly 60% to just 18%. The dollar index ticked up 0.42% to ~102.3 regardless, and the yield spike was the day's dominant story over the minutes themselves.

🛢️ Hormuz Remains Effectively Closed as Tanker Attacks Hit a War-High Pace

WTI held near $90.09 as the Strait of Hormuz standoff stays acute — only ~4 tanker transits were recorded on Oct 4 versus a normal ~85/day, and attacks on tankers hit their highest weekly level since the conflict began. Iran's parliament speaker says the strait stays shut until seven conditions are met; Trump says a decision is imminent "the easy way or the hard way." Gold fell 1.32% to $4,109.61 even with this backdrop, likely profit-taking after its recent run plus the stronger dollar.

🥤 PepsiCo Opens Earnings Season In-Line; China Reopens After Golden Week

PepsiCo reported Q3 EPS of $2.30, in line with (fractionally above) the $2.29-2.30 consensus, reaffirming FY26 organic revenue growth guidance of 2-4%. Frito-Lay/North America volume and margin pressure remains the core overhang; the post-earnings stock reaction was not yet confirmed as of this writing — worth checking at the open. Separately, China's mainland markets reopened today after the Golden Week holiday, following a rocky lead-in (Hang Seng fell 2.6% on Oct 2, its worst day since March) on global-yield and Hormuz-linked stress, partly offset by a new 1-point mortgage-rate subsidy effective Oct 1.

🧠 Trusted Investor Insights MACRO VIEWS
Goldman Sachs — Macro Research

"Yield Ahead": The Era of Central-Bank Yield Suppression Is Over

Goldman's Oct 6 "Market Know-How 4Q 2026" report argues bonds have reclaimed their role as the market's pricing arbiter — directly relevant after Wednesday's 24-year-high intraday yield print. The note flags "HEAT" risks (Harvests, Energy, AI, Trade) to inflation and expects a shallow DM hiking cycle followed by a prolonged pause. Separately, Goldman projects Q3 2026 S&P 500 EPS growth of ~27% YoY, but warns roughly $150B of "paper gains" inflated Q2 reported earnings — a quality-of-earnings caution worth keeping in mind into this earnings season.

Warren Buffett / Berkshire Hathaway

A Bigger Footprint in Housing, Leadership Transition Complete

Buffett has now stepped down as Chairman as well (son Howard Buffett succeeds him; Greg Abel remains CEO). Berkshire's housing push continues: the Taylor Morrison acquisition makes it America's 4th-largest homebuilder, and its Lennar stake has grown to 11.2%, though the buying pace has slowed to roughly $18M/day.

Ray Dalio / Bridgewater

Warns of a Possible US Debt Crisis Within Three Years

In an Oct 6 Bloomberg TV interview from Singapore, Dalio warned that China and Japan — key foreign buyers of US Treasuries — may keep pulling back demand, risking a debt crisis within roughly three years. He recommends being short duration and long gold, suggesting a 5-15% portfolio gold allocation, and separately said markets have entered "bubble territory" — but advises against selling into it.

Howard Marks / Oaktree Capital

Rising Yields Are "Not a Crisis, It's an Invoice"

Marks's latest memo argues the rise in long-term yields is a rational response to sticky inflation, a roughly 6%-of-GDP peacetime deficit, and AI-driven capital demand — framing the 30-year yield near 5.5% as the bill finally coming due for years of fiscal and monetary largesse, rather than a market malfunction. A separate recent memo covers AI/autonomous agents and their implications for knowledge workers and investors.

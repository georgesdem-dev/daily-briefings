💻 Nasdaq's Worst Day Since June as AI-Spending Fears Hammer Chips

Thursday's session delivered the sharpest AI-trade reversal in months: the Nasdaq Composite tumbled 2.60% to 26,008.86, its steepest daily drop since June 5, as renewed concern over the pace and payoff of AI infrastructure spending triggered a broad selloff in technology and semiconductor names. Intel, AMD, Sandisk and Marvell Technology each fell more than 8%. The S&P 500 fell 1.21% to 7,663.43 — its worst day since July — while the Dow, with less tech weighting, eked out a marginal 0.02% gain to 52,867.98.

The move follows Wednesday's mixed Meta/Tesla earnings reaction and builds on a week of growing skepticism about AI-linked capex discipline. Ray Dalio's early-October warning that AI is a "classic bubble" nearing a bursting point — citing roughly $570B in AI-linked debt issuance for 2026, more than double last year's total — and Howard Marks's more measured "inflection bubble" framing both look more timely after Thursday's reversal.

🔄 Flight to Safety: Staples, Utilities and Healthcare Lead the Rotation

Investors rotated hard out of growth and into classic defensives Thursday. Consumer Staples gained 3.31% — the day's top-performing sector — as traders pulled money out of AI-infrastructure names and into companies selling toothpeste, soap and groceries. Utilities rose 2.07%, Health Care added 1.32%, Real Estate and Financials each gained around 1.09%, and Materials rose 0.72%. On the other side, Consumer Discretionary fell 0.45% and Energy slipped 0.87% even as oil prices climbed — a reminder that commodity price moves and energy-equity moves don't always track together day to day.

🧠 Trusted Investor Insights MACRO VIEWS
Ray Dalio / Bridgewater

"Classic Bubble" Warning Looks Prescient After Nasdaq's Worst Day Since June

Dalio's early-October remarks in Singapore that AI is a "classic bubble" nearing its bursting point — driven by rising rates colliding with roughly $570B of AI-linked debt issuance in 2026, more than double 2025's total — read as considerably more timely after Thursday's chip-and-megacap-tech rout. He flagged two triggers: continued rate pressure, and forced liquidation of unrealized gains (e.g. via wealth taxes). Thursday's selloff, paired with lower Treasury yields from the flight to bonds, shows the first dynamic already in motion.

Howard Marks / Oaktree Capital

"Inflection Bubble," Not a Confirmed One — Moderation Over Conviction

Marks continues to resist calling the AI boom a classic bubble outright, instead describing an "inflection bubble" shaped by over $1B in seed funding flowing to unproven startups and heavy corporate debt tied to infrastructure buildout. His standing advice remains relevant today: no one should go all-in without acknowledging the risk of ruin, but no one should go all-out and miss a genuine technological step forward either. "A moderate position, applied with selectivity and prudence, seems like the best approach" — exactly the stance this portfolio's defensive/growth split is built around.

Goldman Sachs — Still Bullish on 2026, Even After Thursday's Drop

8,000 S&P Target Unchanged; October Conviction List (AMZN, OXY, HII, JCI, Burlington) Holds

Goldman's year-end S&P 500 target of 8,000 (raised from 7,600 earlier this year, on the back of a $340 2026 EPS forecast and 24% earnings growth) was set well before Thursday's tech-driven pullback but hasn't been walked back. The desk's view that earnings growth — not multiple expansion — drives the next leg higher is directly tested by a week where even strong earners (Meta) got punished for spending decisions. The October Conviction List, including today's strong performers OXY and HII, remains the anchor for this portfolio's picks.

Warren Buffett / Berkshire Hathaway

"Never a More Gambling Mood" — Patience Remains the Strategy

Buffett's recent comments that "we've never had people in a more gambling mood than now," pointing specifically to the explosion in one-day options trading, look well-timed given Thursday's violent single-day rotation. Berkshire's cash pile — $344.1B at Q2-end — stays on the sidelines; Buffett has said only a "big" decline (not a mild one, as seen earlier this year) would trigger deployment. Thursday's 1.2% S&P drop likely still falls short of that threshold, but it's a reminder of how quickly sentiment can turn when AI-spending assumptions get questioned.
